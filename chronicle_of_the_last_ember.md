# The Chronicle of the Last Ember

*Being a fragment recovered from the Archives of Hethar, believed to date from the late Third Silence.*

They say the mountains remember the first songs, but we have forgotten the words. We know only the hum of the stone when the wind blows from the North, a sound like a great and weary sigh.

In those days, the Shadow did not come with fire or armies. It came as a reasonable suggestion. "Why labor?" it whispered to the masons. "Why sing?" it asked the weavers. "Why remember?" it murmured to the scribes. And one by one, the great works ceased, not destroyed, but abandoned. The towers were not toppled; they were simply left unfinished. The songs were not forbidden; they were merely forgotten, replaced by silence and the practical noise of commerce.

Eamon was no hero. He was a mender of nets in the coastal village of Oakhaven. His hands were rough, his back bent from years of leaning over the fraying cords. He knew the sea, and he knew the knots that held against the tide. He did not know magic, for magic was a heavy thing, a burden for the highborn and the doomed.

But Eamon remembered a story his grandmother had told him, a fragment of a verse about the Light that held the world together. It was said that the Light was not a sun, nor a star, but a small, stubborn coal kept burning in the heart of the mountain, tended by the Faithful.

"Faithfulness," his grandmother had said, "is not a feeling. It is a long obedience in the same direction."

One evening, a stranger came to Oakhaven. He wore grey, and his eyes were the color of rain. He did not speak of the Shadow, but of the cold that was creeping into the hearts of men. He spoke of the fire in the mountain, and how it was dying, not for lack of fuel, but for lack of belief.

"Who will go?" the stranger asked. The village elders looked at their feet. The young warriors looked at their swords, wondering if steel could cut silence. The rich merchants looked at their ledgers.

Eamon looked at his nets. He saw the mend he had just finished, a simple, strong knot that would hold against the grey waves. He thought of his grandmother's voice.

"I will go," Eamon said. "Not because I am strong, but because I am here."

The stranger nodded. "The road is long, and the price is high. You will carry nothing but your hope, and you may lose even that."

Eamon set out the next morning. He took no sword, only a walking stick and a small lantern. The journey was not filled with battles, but with weariness. The wind bit through his cloak. The road was lonely. The silence of the world pressed against his mind, whispering that his quest was foolish, that the fire was already out, that he should return to his nets and sleep.

He met others on the way. Elara, a singer whose voice had been stolen by grief. Kael, a guardian whose shield was rusted. They joined him, not out of bravery, but out of a shared brokenness. They walked together, sharing their meager food and their doubts.

"Why do we continue?" Elara asked one night, the cold seeping into their bones.

"Because the story is not yet over," Eamon answered. "And because someone must remember."

They reached the mountain at last. It was not a place of glory, but of desolate stone. The entrance was hidden, a crack in the rock that only those who looked with humility could find. Inside, the air was stale and cold.

They descended into the dark. The shadows here were thick and heavy, pulling at their limbs. Kael fell behind, his strength failing. Elara's song faltered. Eamon walked on, his lantern a tiny speck of defiance in the crushing dark.

At the heart of the mountain, they found the Chamber of the Ember. It was a vast, silent hall. In the center lay a great hearth, cold and grey. But in the ashes, a single spark still glowed, faint and fading.

The Guardian of the Hearth was gone. Only dust remained.

"It is too late," Kael whispered. "It is dead."

Eamon knelt by the hearth. He felt the cold radiating from the stone. He thought of the world outside, the grey silence, the forgetting. He thought of his nets, and the knots that held.

"Magic is a cost," the stranger had said.

Eamon understood then. The fire did not need wood. It needed life. It needed a heart that was willing to burn.

"I have nothing to give," Elara wept.

"We have ourselves," Eamon said softly.

He reached out and took the spark in his bare hand. The pain was immediate and absolute, a searing agony that tore through his flesh. But he did not let go. He held it, shielding it from the cold air, breathing his own life onto it.

He began to sing, a rough, broken song—the verse his grandmother had taught him. Elara joined him, her voice gathering strength from his sacrifice. Kael stood guard, his rusted shield raised against the encroaching dark.

The spark flared. It fed on Eamon's pain, on his endurance, on his love for the simple, mended things of the world. It grew into a flame, then a fire, golden and pure. It did not roar; it sang.

The light spread, filling the chamber, warming the stone, flowing out through the cracks in the mountain. It washed over the land, not a conquering sun, but a gentle, healing dawn.

Eamon looked at the fire, his hand charred and ruined, his face pale. He smiled.

"It holds," he whispered.

And he closed his eyes.

They say the fire still burns, tended now by the order of the Menders, who wear the badge of a knotted net. They say the Shadow still lingers in the corners of the world, waiting for us to forget. But as long as one person remembers the story of Eamon, the light will not go out.

*End of the fragment.*
